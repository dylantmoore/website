What Can Bunching Teach Us About Tagging?

Identifying Preference Heterogeneity from Tax Kinks

Dylan T. Moore
University of Hawaii at Manoa

The Central Question

Can we use bunching behavior at tax kinks to identify whether differentiated commodity taxation is warranted?

Key Insight

Discontinuities in consumption patterns at tax bracket thresholds reveal preference heterogeneity needed for optimal tagging

Surprising Finding

Point identification of tagging-relevant parameters is possible even when the ETI is not identified from bunching alone

Why This Matters

Standard bunching literature:

Focuses on identifying elasticity of taxable income (ETI) from bunching mass

This paper:

Looks at other consumption choices (good $x$) around tax kinks

These patterns reveal whether different income groups have different preferences

This information determines optimal commodity tax differentiation

Model Setup: Agent Types

A continuum of agents, each characterized by type $(w, \theta)$:

Productivity Dimension

$w \in W \subseteq \mathbb{R}_{++}$

Captures earning ability / wage

Preference Dimension

$\theta \in \Theta$

Preference heterogeneity beyond productivity

$\Theta$ can be discrete, continuous, or mixed

Population distribution: $f_w(w|\theta)$ with measure $\mu$ over $\Theta$

The Agent's Problem

Agent of type $(w,\theta)$ chooses:

  • $z$: taxable income
  • $x$: consumption of some differentiated good
  • $c$: consumption of numeraire good

Maximizes utility:

$$\max_{z,x,c} \quad u(c, x, z; w, \theta)$$

Subject to budget constraint:

$$c + x + \mathcal{T}_x(x) = z - \mathcal{T}_z(z)$$

Two-Stage Optimization

Stage 2: Given income $z$, choose $x$

$$x(w,\theta;z) = \arg\max_{x} u(z - \mathcal{T}_z(z) - x - \mathcal{T}_x(x), x, z; w, \theta)$$

Stage 1: Choose optimal income $z$

$$z(w,\theta) = \arg\max_{z} u(z - \mathcal{T}_z(z) - x(w,\theta;z) - \mathcal{T}_x(x(w,\theta;z)), x(w,\theta;z), z; w, \theta)$$

Actual consumption: $x(w,\theta) \equiv x(w,\theta; z(w,\theta))$

First-Order Conditions

Stage 2 FOC: Choosing $x$ given $z$

$$1 + \mathcal{T}_x'(x) = \frac{u_x}{u_c}$$

Marginal rate of substitution equals relative price

Stage 1 FOC: Choosing $z$

$$1 - \mathcal{T}_z'(z) = -\frac{u_z}{u_c}$$

Marginal rate of substitution between income and consumption

Optimal Commodity Tax Differentiation

Classic result (Atkinson-Stiglitz):

Uniform commodity taxation is optimal when preferences are homogeneous across types

Recent framework (Ferey, Lockwood, & Taubinsky 2024):

Rate differentiation is proportional to residual Engel curve variation not explained by causal income effects

Question: Can we identify these residuals from observable data?

Quasi-Distribution Neutral Reforms

Definition:

A reform where the expected mechanical change in tax payments is zero at each income level

$$\tau_z(z) = -\mathbb{E}[\tau_x(x(w,\theta)) \mid z(w,\theta) = z]$$

This isolates behavioral responses from mechanical revenue effects

Quasi-Distribution Neutral Reforms

Intuition for linear commodity tax:

Increase tax on $x$ by $\kappa$

Reduce income tax at each $z$ by $\bar{x}'(z) \cdot \kappa$

where $\bar{x}'(z) = \mathbb{E}[x(w,\theta) \mid z(w,\theta) = z]$ is average consumption at income $z$

Result: On average, taxpayers at each income level can still afford their original bundle

Optimal Linear Commodity Tax

Under QDN reforms, the optimal tax rate $t_x$ satisfies:

$$t_x = \frac{\int \mathcal{T}_z'(z) \frac{1+t_x}{1-\mathcal{T}_z'(z)} \left(\frac{\bar{x}'(z)\bar{\varepsilon}_z(z) - \mathbb{E}[x'_{inc}(z;\theta)\varepsilon_z(z;\theta)|z]}{\mathbb{E}[x(z;\theta)\varepsilon_{x|z}(z;\theta)]}\right) z h_z(z) dz}{1 + \int \frac{1+t_x}{1-\mathcal{T}_z'(z)} \left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[x'^2_{inc}(z;\theta)\varepsilon_z(z;\theta)|z] - \bar{x}'(z)\mathbb{E}[x'_{inc}(z;\theta)\varepsilon_z(z;\theta)|z]}{\mathbb{E}[x(z;\theta)\varepsilon_{x|z}(z;\theta)]}\right) z h_z(z) dz}$$

Key term: $\mathbb{E}[x'_{inc}(z;\theta)\varepsilon_z(z;\theta)|z]$

This is a sufficient statistic for optimal policy

Can we identify it from observable data?

The Setting: Tax Bracket Thresholds

Consider a piecewise linear income tax:

$$T(z) = \begin{cases} t_0 z & \text{if } z \leq z^* \\ t_1(z - z^*) + t_0 z^* & \text{if } z > z^* \end{cases}$$

where $t_1 > t_0$ and $z^*$ is the bracket threshold

Key observation: At $z^*$, we observe two different groups:

  • Those just below (facing rate $t_0$)
  • Those just above (facing rate $t_1$)

Consumption Discontinuity at the Kink

Let $\bar{x}_o(z)$ be the observed average consumption of $x$ at income $z$

At the kink point $z^*$, we observe:

$$\lim_{z \to^- z^*} \bar{x}_o(z) = \bar{x}(z^*; t_0)$$

$$\lim_{z \to^+ z^*} \bar{x}_o(z) = \bar{x}(z^*; t_1)$$

The discontinuity:

$$\Delta \bar{x} = \lim_{z \to^+ z^*} \bar{x}_o(z) - \lim_{z \to^- z^*} \bar{x}_o(z)$$

Why This Discontinuity Matters

Naive interpretation (wrong!):

The jump reveals cross-price elasticity between $z$ and $x$

Problem:

Agent types are not continuous across the threshold

Different people locate just below vs. just above $z^*$

Key insight:

This compositional change actually reveals exactly what we need for tagging!

Decomposing the Discontinuity

The change in average consumption can be written as:

$$\frac{\partial \bar{x}(z^*;t)}{\partial t} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial x(z^*,\theta;t)}{\partial t} \mid z=z^*\right]}_{\text{Direct effect}} + \underbrace{\mathbb{C}\left[x(z^*,\theta;t), \frac{\partial \ln h(z^*|\theta;t)}{\partial t} \mid z=z^*\right]}_{\text{Composition effect}}$$

Direct effect: How individuals change their $x$ consumption in response to tax rate changes

Composition effect: How the types of people at $z^*$ change when the tax rate changes

The Heterogeneity Term

Define the heterogeneity-driven variation in consumption:

$$x'_{het}(z^*,\theta;t) \equiv \left[\frac{\partial x}{\partial w} / \frac{\partial z}{\partial w}\right]_{w=w^{-1}(z^*)} \cdot [x(z^*,\theta;t) - \bar{x}(z^*;t)] \cdot \frac{h'(z^*|\theta;t)}{h(z^*|\theta;t)}$$

This captures the non-causal part of the relationship between $x$ and $z$:

  • How $x$ varies with type, holding income constant
  • Scaled by how types sort across income levels

Main Result

Under certain conditions (when $\beta = 0$), the consumption discontinuity identifies:

$$\frac{\lim_{z \to^+ z^*} \bar{x}_o(z) - \lim_{z \to^- z^*} \bar{x}_o(z)}{t_1 - t_0} = \frac{1}{t_1 - t_0} \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \frac{z^*}{1-t} \mathbb{E}[x'_{het}(z^*,\theta;t) \varepsilon^c(z^*,\theta;t) \mid z=z^*] dt$$

What does this mean?

The observable jump in consumption directly identifies a weighted average of the sufficient statistic for optimal tagging

When is $\beta = 0$?

The bias term $\beta$ equals zero when:

Condition 1: Constant ETI

The elasticity of taxable income $\varepsilon^c(z^*,\theta;t)$ is constant across $\theta$ (given $z$)

Condition 2: Independence

The ETI is uncorrelated with consumption of $x$ conditional on $z$

Key point: These are much weaker conditions than needed to identify the ETI from bunching!

A Remarkable Result

Standard bunching literature:

Cannot identify ETI without strong assumptions about counterfactual income distribution

This result:

Point identifies a policy-relevant sufficient statistic using only observable data

No need to estimate counterfactual distributions

Works even when ETI is not identified!

The "holy grail" of bunching identification — but for tagging parameters!

Economic Interpretation

What are we learning from the consumption jump?

1. Preference heterogeneity:

Do people with similar incomes have systematically different tastes for $x$?

2. Sorting patterns:

How do these preference differences correlate with income choices?

3. Behavioral responses:

How sensitive are income choices to tax rate changes?

All three ingredients → optimal commodity tax differentiation

Closing the Loop: Back to Optimal Policy

Recall the optimal commodity tax formula required:

$$\mathbb{E}[x'_{inc}(z;\theta)\varepsilon_z(z;\theta)|z]$$

Our identification result gives us:

$$\mathbb{E}[x'_{het}(z^*,\theta;t) \varepsilon^c(z^*,\theta;t) \mid z=z^*]$$

Since $x'_{het}(z;\theta) = \bar{x}'(z) - x'_{inc}(z;\theta)$, these are closely related!

With additional data on $\bar{x}'(z)$, we can back out the causal income effect

Empirical Implementation

What you need:

  • Tax schedule with kinks (e.g., graduated income tax)
  • Data on taxable income $z$
  • Data on consumption of some good $x$ (e.g., from scanner data, administrative records)

What you do:

  • Estimate $\bar{x}_o(z)$ around tax bracket thresholds
  • Measure the discontinuity at each kink
  • Scale by the tax rate difference

What you get:

Direct estimate of whether commodity tax differentiation is warranted

Extensions and Future Work

Current paper establishes:

  • Identification at tax kinks
  • Linear commodity taxes
  • Point identification of sufficient statistics

Natural extensions:

  • Nonlinear income tax schedules
  • Tax notches (instead of kinks)
  • Multiple commodities
  • Estimation strategies for empirical application

Conclusion

Key contributions:

  • Formal framework linking bunching analysis to optimal tagging
  • Point identification of policy-relevant sufficient statistics from consumption discontinuities
  • Identification possible even when ETI cannot be identified from bunching alone
  • Clear guidance for empirical implementation

Looking beyond bunching masses to consumption patterns
reveals the heterogeneity structure needed for optimal policy design
