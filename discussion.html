Robust Benchmarks in Optimal Taxation

How Parameter Uncertainty Shapes Tax Policy

Dylan T. Moore, Ashley C. Craig, & Thomas Lloyd

The Central Question

How should tax policy be designed when there is uncertainty about key parameters?

Key Insight

Models with multidimensional heterogeneity can be reinterpreted as models of parameter uncertainty

Surprising Finding

Uncertainty can make simple benchmarks optimal ex ante, even when they wouldn't be optimal ex post

The Classic Atkinson-Stiglitz Result

Uniform commodity taxation is optimal when:

  • Preferences over consumption goods are weakly separable from labor supply
  • Consumption subutility functions are homogeneous across agents

Violation → Tagging Opportunity

If agents have different tastes, differentiated commodity taxes can serve as an indirect redistribution mechanism

The Tagging Rationale

Recent Framework (Ferey et al. 2024):

Optimal rate differentiation is proportional to the residual portion of consumption Engel curves not explained by causal income effects

Different tastes across types → Differential taxation can improve efficiency

But what if the planner is uncertain about these taste differences?

Model Setup: Two Interpretations

Heterogeneity Model

Agents have types $(w, \theta)$

Distribution: $F_W(w|\theta)$

Planner knows joint distribution

Uncertainty Model

Agents have types $w$

State $\theta$ is uncertain

Planner has beliefs $\mu$ over $\theta$

Key equivalence: As long as budget constraint holds in expectation,
these models are mathematically identical but conceptually different

The Parametric Model

We work with a specific utility function:

$$u(c,x,z;w) = \frac{c^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} + \alpha(w)\frac{x^{1-\sigma}}{1-\sigma} - \psi\frac{(z/w)^{1+1/\varepsilon}}{1+1/\varepsilon}$$

Consumption Utility

$c^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$: Standard CRRA

γ = risk aversion parameter

Commodity Utility

$\alpha(w) x^{1-\sigma}/(1-\sigma)$: Taste for good $x$

σ = curvature parameter

α(w) = taste that varies with wage

Labor Disutility

$\psi (z/w)^{1+1/\varepsilon}/(1+1/\varepsilon)$

ψ = disutility intensity

ε = Frisch elasticity

Taste Heterogeneity

$\alpha(w) = \alpha_0 \cdot w^\beta$

α₀ = base taste intensity

β = wage elasticity of taste

The Agent's Problem

Agent of type $w$ in state $\theta$ maximizes:

$$u(c, x, z; w, \theta)$$

subject to budget constraint:

$$c + x + \mathcal{T}_x(x) = z - \mathcal{T}_z(z)$$

$z$ = taxable income, $x$ = differentiated commodity, $c$ = numeraire consumption

Two-Stage Optimization

Stage 2: Given income $z$, choose $x$

$$x(w,\theta;z) = \arg\max_{x} u(z - \mathcal{T}_z(z) - x - \mathcal{T}_x(x), x, z; w, \theta)$$

Stage 1: Choose optimal income $z$

$$z(w,\theta) = \arg\max_{z} u(z - \mathcal{T}_z(z) - x(w,\theta;z) - \mathcal{T}_x(x(w,\theta;z)), x(w,\theta;z), z; w, \theta)$$

Understanding the First-Order Conditions

The two-stage structure leads to two distinct FOCs:

Stage 2 FOC: Choosing $x$ given $z$

$$u_c \cdot (1 + \mathcal{T}_x'(x)) = u_x$$

Stage 1 FOC: Choosing $z$ (accounting for induced $x$ changes)

$$u_c \left(1 - \mathcal{T}_z'(z) - \frac{\partial x}{\partial z}(1+\mathcal{T}_x'(x))\right) + u_x \frac{\partial x}{\partial z} = -u_z$$

Let's visualize these graphically...

Visualizing the First-Order Conditions

Stage 2: Choice of x

$$u_c (1+\tau_x) = u_x$$

Stage 1: Choice of z

$$1-\tau_z - \frac{\partial x}{\partial z}(1+\tau_x) = -\frac{u_z}{u_c}$$

Testing Atkinson-Stiglitz: Model Comparison

We'll compare how choices respond to a wage increase from W = 2 → 3 under two models:

Model A: β = 0

α(w) = α₀ (constant)

Atkinson-Stiglitz: Satisfied ✓

Commodity taste independent of wage

Model B: β > 0

α(w) = α₀ · w^β

Atkinson-Stiglitz: Violated ✗

Higher wage → higher taste for x

Do they respond differently to wage changes?

Model A: β = 0 (Atkinson-Stiglitz Satisfied)

Key Property:

$$\alpha(w) = \alpha_0$$

Taste for x is constant

Stage 2 FOC:

$$u_c (1+\tau_x) = \alpha_0 x^{-\sigma}$$

MRS curve does not shift with W

Effect of Wage Increase: W = 2 → W = 3

Higher W → higher income → move along same MRS curve → more x

Model B: β > 0 (Atkinson-Stiglitz Violated)

Key Property:

$$\alpha(w) = \alpha_0 w^\beta$$

Taste for x increases with W

Stage 2 FOC:

$$u_c (1+\tau_x) = \alpha_0 w^\beta x^{-\sigma}$$

MRS curve shifts up with W

Effect of Wage Increase: W = 2 → W = 3

Higher W → both higher income AND higher taste → much more x

Characterizing Optimal Policy

To find optimal tax policy, we consider marginal tax reforms

Key Challenge:

With multidimensional heterogeneity (or uncertainty), we can't construct fully distribution-neutral reforms

Characterizing Optimal Policy

Quasi-Distribution Neutral (QDN) Reforms

A reform where the expected mechanical change in tax payments is zero at each income level:

$$\tau_z(z) = -\mathbb{E}[\tau_x(x(w,\theta)) \mid z(w,\theta) = z]$$

This isolates behavioral responses from mechanical revenue effects

QDN Reform: Economic Intuition

Consider a linear commodity tax reform: increase $t_x$ by 1 unit

QDN condition requires: Reduce income tax at each $z$ by $\bar{x}'(z)$

where $\bar{x}'(z) = \mathbb{E}[x(w,\theta) \mid z(w,\theta) = z]$

is the average consumption of $x$ at income level $z$

Result: On average, taxpayers at each income level can still afford their original bundle

The Key Equation: Setup

Consider the first-stage FOC for choosing $z$:

$u_c$ $($ $1$ $- \mathcal{T}_z'(z)$ $- \frac{\partial x}{\partial z}(1+t_x)$ $)$ $+ u_x \frac{\partial x}{\partial z}$ $=$ $-u_z$

Note: $\frac{\partial x}{\partial z}(1+t_x)$ represents how the commodity tax effectively alters the marginal income tax rate

The Key Equation: Rearrangement

Rearranging the FOC:

$1$ $- \mathcal{T}_z'(z)$ $- \frac{\partial x}{\partial z}(1+t_x)$ $=$ $-\frac{u_z}{u_c}$ $- \frac{\partial x}{\partial z}\frac{u_x}{u_c}$

Under a QDN reform (increase $t_x$ by $\kappa$, reduce $\mathcal{T}_z'(z)$ by $\bar{x}'(z)\kappa$):

$1$ $- \mathcal{T}_z'(z)$ $+ \bar{x}'(z)\kappa$ $- \frac{\partial x}{\partial z}(1+t_x+\kappa)$ $=$ $-\frac{u_z}{u_c}$ $- \frac{\partial x}{\partial z}\frac{u_x}{u_c}$

The Key Equation: Heterogeneity Term

Simplifying the QDN-reformed FOC:

$$1 - \mathcal{T}_z'(z) - \frac{\partial x}{\partial z}(1+t_x) + \underbrace{\left[\bar{x}'(z) - \frac{\partial x}{\partial z}\right]}_{\equiv \bar{x}'_{het}(z;\theta)}\kappa = -\frac{u_z}{u_c} - \frac{\partial x}{\partial z}\frac{u_x}{u_c}$$

Key insight: A marginal QDN reform decreases the effective marginal income tax rate by $\bar{x}'_{het}(z;\theta)$

where $\bar{x}'_{het}(z;\theta) = \bar{x}'(z) - x'_{inc}(z;\theta)$

$\bar{x}'(z)$ = average marginal propensity to consume $x$ at income $z$
$x'_{inc}(z;\theta)$ = causal income effect in state $\theta$

Behavioral Responses to QDN Reform

Expected increase in taxable income:

$$\frac{z\varepsilon_z(z;\theta)}{1-\mathcal{T}_z'(z)} \bar{x}'_{het}(z;\theta)$$

This generates revenue from TWO sources:

1. Direct income tax revenue:

$$\mathcal{T}_z'(z) \cdot \frac{z\varepsilon_z(z;\theta)}{1-\mathcal{T}_z'(z)} \bar{x}'_{het}(z;\theta)$$

2. Cross-base effects (change in $z$ induces change in $x$):

$$t_x \cdot x'_{inc}(z;\theta) \cdot \frac{z\varepsilon_z(z;\theta)}{1-\mathcal{T}_z'(z)} \bar{x}'_{het}(z;\theta)$$

↑ Extra z → Extra x → Extra tax on x

Optimal Policy Condition

At the optimum, marginal QDN reforms must have zero welfare effect:

Marginal Cost (efficiency loss from taxing $x$):

$$t_x \cdot \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\varepsilon_{x|z}(z;\theta) x(z;\theta)}{1+t_x}\right]$$

Marginal Benefit (revenue from behavioral responses):

$$\int \mathcal{T}_z'(z) \frac{\bar{\varepsilon}_z(z) z}{1-\mathcal{T}_z'(z)} \bar{x}'_{het}(z) h_z(z) dz + \int t_x \mathbb{E}\left[x'_{inc}(z;\theta) \frac{\varepsilon_z(z;\theta) z}{1-\mathcal{T}_z'(z)} \bar{x}'_{het}(z) \mid z\right] h_z(z) dz$$

Key point: This is not just the expected value of components — covariances matter!

Where Does Uncertainty Enter?

Expanding the optimal policy condition reveals covariance terms:

$$t_x = \frac{\text{benefit from using mean parameters}}{\text{cost from using mean parameters} + \text{uncertainty penalty}}$$

Where Does Uncertainty Enter?

The full expression:

$$t_x = \frac{\int \mathcal{T}_z'(z) \frac{\bar{\varepsilon}_z(z) z}{1-\mathcal{T}_z'(z)} \bar{x}'_{het}(z) h_z(z) dz}{\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\varepsilon_{x|z} x}{1+t_x}\right] - \int \bar{x}'_{inc}(z) \frac{\bar{\varepsilon}_z(z) z}{1-\mathcal{T}_z'(z)} \bar{x}'_{het}(z) h_z(z) dz + \color{#A23B72}{\int \frac{\bar{\varepsilon}_z(z) z}{1-\mathcal{T}_z'(z)} \mathbb{V}[x'_{inc}(z;\theta) \mid z] h_z(z) dz}}$$

The variance term in the denominator represents the uncertainty penalty

The Variance Attenuation Effect

Why does variance in $x'_{inc}$ push toward uniformity?

The causal income effect $x'_{inc}(z;\theta)$ plays two opposing roles:

1. Determines how much the incentive to earn changes under QDN reform

via $\bar{x}'_{het}(z;\theta) = \bar{x}'(z) - x'_{inc}(z;\theta)$

2. Determines the cross-base response of $x$ to changes in $z$

$$\mathbb{C}[x'_{inc}(z;\theta), \bar{x}'_{het}(z;\theta) \mid z] = -\mathbb{V}[x'_{inc}(z;\theta) \mid z] < 0$$

Intuition: When incentive changes are large, behavioral responses are small, and vice versa

Main Result

As uncertainty about causal income effects grows,
optimal commodity taxation approaches the
Atkinson-Stiglitz uniform benchmark

This is NOT due to:

  • Planner risk aversion
  • Inability to tagging in any particular state

This IS due to:

  • Negative covariance between incentive changes and behavioral responses
  • Fundamental tension in the dual role of income effects

Reinterpreting Atkinson-Stiglitz

Traditional view:

Uniform commodity taxation follows from strong functional form assumptions about preferences

New perspective:

Uniformity can arise from informational considerations

Rate differentiation may be attractive in principle, but planner uncertainty undermines its appeal in practice

A robust benchmark emerges from uncertainty, not from strong assumptions

Broader Implications

Not all benchmarks are reinforced by uncertainty:

Pigouvian taxation:

• Atmospheric externalities ex post
• But ex ante, all externalities are non-atmospheric
• Uncertainty complicates optimal corrective taxation

General principle:

Parameter uncertainty can either simplify or complicate optimal policy, depending on the structure of the problem

Conclusion

Key contributions:

  • Reinterpret multidimensional heterogeneity models as uncertainty models
  • Characterize optimal policy under parameter uncertainty using QDN reforms
  • Show that uncertainty creates systematic force toward simple benchmarks in commodity taxation
  • Provide new perspective on classic Atkinson-Stiglitz result

When planners are uncertain about the world,
robust simplicity can dominate tailored complexity
